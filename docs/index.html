Definition 1.1 (ePortfolio). A curated collection of student-created work — for learning, for assessment, and for showcasing what you can do — with reflection on each artifact and on the connections between them. Selection. Reflection. Connection.

Corollary (Math Ed Portfolio). The same thing, organized around what a beginning mathematics teacher needs to be able to do. Without the reflection it is just a website or a repository. With it, it is the argument, with evidence, that you are ready for your own classroom.

Why a portfolio

One place where the pieces of the program add up

Right now your best work is scattered across Canvas courses, Google Drive folders, and a cooperating teacher's memory. The portfolio collects it and makes it usable.

∴ Stronger job applications

Principals hiring math teachers want to see a lesson you taught and how you responded to what students did. You will have both, with video, ready to share.

∴ Learning that transfers

What you learn in a content course rarely carries into a methods course, or a classroom, on its own. Reflecting on each artifact — what this task is like and not like what you've done before — is how it does.

∴ A habit of reflection

Writing two paragraphs about why a lesson on slope fell apart is how you get better at teaching slope. The portfolio makes that a routine, not an assignment.

What goes in

Seven kinds of artifacts, each with a reflection

Most of these come out of courses you are already taking. The portfolio asks you to choose your best example, annotate it, and keep it.

Teaching philosophy

One page on what you believe about how students learn mathematics. Drafted early, revised every year — the revisions are the interesting part.

revised yearly

Lesson plans

Two to three full plans across grade bands and content strands — say, a middle-school proportional reasoning lesson and a high-school proof lesson — with standards alignment and anticipated student responses.

methods courses

Analysis of student work

Real (anonymized) student responses to a task you gave, sorted by strategy and misconception, with what you would do next. The heart of the portfolio.

field placement

Classroom video

A 10–15 minute clip of you teaching, with time-stamped commentary on the questions you asked and the decisions you made in the moment.

student teaching

Assessment design

A quiz, task, or rubric you wrote, plus the results and what they told you. Shows you can measure understanding, not just assign points.

methods courses

Mathematical depth

A piece of your own mathematics — a proof, a modeling project, an exploration — that shows you know the content well beyond the level you will teach.

math courses

Professional growth record

Observations, feedback from cooperating teachers and supervisors, conferences attended, and your responses to each. Evidence that you take feedback and use it.

ongoing

Every artifact gets a reflection — and the reflection points at the artifact.

Process

What surprised or challenged you? What would you do differently, and why?

Outcomes

Point to the place in this artifact where you show you can do X.

Integration

How does this connect to earlier courses, placements, or your own schooling?

Development

How have you grown as a mathematics teacher since the last version?

Timeline

Built in step with the program, not on top of it

Each year adds two or three artifacts. Nothing is due all at once, and every piece is reviewed with a faculty mentor before it goes in.

Year 1

Start the record

  • Create your Google Site shell in UNV 101
  • First reflection: who are you, and who do you want to become as a teacher?
  • Draft a teaching philosophy; add one piece of your own mathematics
Year 2

First classroom evidence

  • Early field-experience observation notes
  • First lesson plan from a methods course
  • Revise the philosophy statement
Year 3

Teach and analyze

  • Second lesson plan in a different grade band
  • Student-work analysis from your placement
  • An assessment you designed and gave
Year 4

Student teaching and defense

  • Classroom video with commentary from student teaching
  • Final philosophy and growth record
  • Concluding reflection in Capstone; submit to MILO and present to faculty and peers

Questions

What students usually ask

Is this extra work on top of my courses?
Mostly no. Nearly every artifact is something a course already asks you to produce. The additional work is choosing your best example and writing a short reflection — roughly an evening per artifact, a few times a year.
Where does the portfolio live?
In Google Sites — the same site as your MILE portfolio, which you create in UNV 101. You own it and keep it after graduation. You don't need to publish it publicly: share it with your mentor and instructors, and remove them when you're done.
How is it graded?
Individual artifacts are reviewed by your faculty mentor with a shared rubric focused on evidence and reflection. The complete portfolio is presented in your final year; the presentation is the capstone, and the portfolio is the evidence for it.
What about student privacy in videos and work samples?
You will use district consent forms for any video, and all student work is anonymized before it goes in. Because the site is shared, not published, only the people you add can see it. Your mentor and cooperating teacher will walk through this before your first placement.
I'm already a junior. Do I start from Year 1?
No. Students joining mid-program build backward from what they already have — most juniors can assemble Years 1 and 2 from existing coursework in a single semester.

Ready to start your portfolio?

Talk to your math education advisor at your next advising appointment, or come to the information session at the start of the semester. Faculty: the implementation page has the eight decisions the program needs to make.
